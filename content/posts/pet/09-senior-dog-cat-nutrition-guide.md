# Senior Dog and Cat Nutrition — When and How to Adjust Feeding as Your Pet Ages

A feeding plan built for a three-year-old dog or cat doesn't automatically stay correct at ten or twelve. Aging changes calorie needs in ways that go in different directions depending on the individual animal — which is exactly why "just feed less as they get older" is an oversimplification that gets it wrong for a meaningful number of senior pets.

## Why Senior Isn't a Single Feeding Rule

The common assumption is that older pets need fewer calories because they're less active — and for many senior pets, that's true. Reduced muscle mass and lower activity levels do lower resting energy needs in a lot of aging animals. But it's not universal:

- **Some senior cats actually need more calories, not fewer.** Aging cats have a documented tendency toward reduced ability to digest and absorb fat and protein efficiently, which can mean an older cat needs a *higher* calorie intake to maintain the same body weight it held at a younger age — the opposite of the "just feed less" instinct.
- **Senior pets with reduced lean muscle mass** can look weight-stable on the scale while actually losing muscle and gaining fat — the number on the scale stays the same, but the body composition behind it has shifted in a way that matters.
- **Some age-related health conditions change calorie needs independently of "aging" itself** — thyroid changes, arthritis-driven activity reduction, and other conditions common in senior pets each push calorie needs in their own direction.

## When Does "Senior" Actually Start?

There's no single universal age cutoff — it varies by species and, for dogs, dramatically by size. Large and giant breed dogs are generally considered senior earlier (sometimes as early as 6–7 years) than small breed dogs, which can remain in an "adult" metabolic profile well into their teens. Cats are typically considered senior starting somewhere around 10–11 years, with a further "geriatric" stage sometimes marked around 15. These are general guideposts, not hard lines — an individual pet's actual condition matters more than a birthday.

## What Actually Should Trigger a Feeding Adjustment

Rather than adjusting feeding based on age alone, the more reliable approach is to adjust based on **observed trend**, checked regularly:

1. **Body weight, tracked consistently** — a gradual unintended weight loss or gain over several consecutive weigh-ins, not a single reading.
2. **Body condition score**, not just weight alone — a senior pet can maintain the same weight while its body condition (fat vs. muscle distribution) shifts.
3. **Activity level changes**, especially ones driven by joint pain or reduced mobility rather than a deliberate choice.
4. **Appetite changes**, particularly in senior cats, where a reduced or increased appetite is often the first visible signal that something in the calorie-need equation has shifted.

## A Common Mistake: Switching to a "Senior" Food and Assuming the Job Is Done

Many senior-formulated commercial foods are lower in calorie density than adult maintenance formulas, on the assumption that senior pets broadly need fewer calories. If that assumption doesn't hold for a specific pet — as it often doesn't for aging cats needing *more*, not fewer, calories — simply switching foods without recalculating the actual daily amount can undercorrect or overcorrect the problem it was meant to solve. The food formulation and the portion amount are two separate decisions, and both need to be checked against the individual pet's actual trend, not assumed together.

Senior pet nutrition isn't a single rule — "feed less" is correct for some aging pets and actively wrong for others, particularly some senior cats. The reliable way to get it right is regular weight and body-condition tracking that catches a gradual trend early, rather than waiting for a visible problem or assuming a birthday alone means it's time to cut portions.
