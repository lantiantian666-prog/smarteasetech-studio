# How Much Water Should a Dog or Cat Drink Each Day?

Water intake gets far less attention than food, but it's just as measurable — and for cats in particular, it's one of the more commonly overlooked aspects of daily care.

## The Basic Formula

A widely used general guideline for daily water needs is approximately **50 ml per kilogram of body weight per day** (roughly 0.8 oz per pound), covering both what a pet drinks directly and, for cats and dogs eating wet or moisture-rich food, what they get from their food. A 4.5 kg (10 lb) cat, for example, has an approximate daily water need in the ballpark of 225 ml — a little under a cup. This is a general guideline, not a precise medical threshold, and actual needs shift with temperature, activity level, and diet.

## Why Cats Are a Special Case

Cats descend from a desert-adapted ancestor and, as a species, tend to have a naturally low thirst drive compared to dogs — they're biologically adapted to get much of their water from prey (which is high in moisture) rather than drinking large amounts directly. In a modern home, especially one where a cat eats mostly dry food, this can translate into a cat that's chronically drinking less than their actual need, without showing any obvious signs of distress day to day.

This matters because low water intake over time is a recognized contributing factor in some feline lower urinary tract conditions and can affect kidney health, particularly as cats age. It's a slow, cumulative pattern rather than an acute emergency in most cases — which is exactly why it's easy to miss without actually tracking it.

## Wet Food vs. Dry Food Changes the Picture

A cat or dog eating primarily wet or raw food is already getting a meaningful share of their water needs from their food itself, since these foods can be 70–80% moisture. A pet eating primarily dry kibble (typically under 10% moisture) is getting almost none of their water need from food, meaning direct drinking has to cover nearly the entire daily requirement. Switching between wet and dry food changes the *effective* amount a pet needs to actively drink, even if their total water need in the formula above stays the same.

## Signs Worth Paying Attention To

- **A noticeable, sustained drop in how much water bowl level changes day to day** — not a single day, but a pattern over a week or more
- **Increased thirst or urination** — this is actually the opposite pattern and can be an early sign of several health conditions, including diabetes and kidney disease, and is worth mentioning to a vet
- **Dry gums or reduced skin elasticity** (a gentle skin-tent test, common in basic at-home hydration checks)
- **A cat that's recently switched from wet to dry food**, or vice versa, without an adjustment period — worth watching more closely during the transition

## Simple Ways to Encourage Adequate Drinking (Especially for Cats)

- Multiple water stations around the home, not just one bowl
- Running water fountains, which some cats are more drawn to than a static bowl
- Wide, shallow bowls — some cats avoid bowls that touch their whiskers repeatedly (whisker fatigue)
- Adding a portion of wet food to an otherwise dry-food diet, if appropriate for the pet's overall plan
