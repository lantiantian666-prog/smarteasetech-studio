# How to Actually Read a Pet Food Label (Most Owners Skip the Number That Matters Most)

Pet food labels are dense, and most of what's printed on them isn't actually what you need for day-to-day feeding decisions. Here's what each section actually means, and which single number matters most for getting portion sizes right.

## The Guaranteed Analysis: Percentages, Not Portions

The "Guaranteed Analysis" panel lists minimum and maximum percentages for crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, and moisture, sometimes with a few additional nutrients. This tells you the food's *composition* — but on its own, it doesn't tell you how much to feed. A food can be "30% protein" and still be wildly different in actual calorie density depending on fat content and moisture.

One subtlety worth knowing: these percentages are usually listed **"as fed"** (including whatever moisture is in the food), which makes direct comparisons between a dry food and a wet food misleading — a wet food's numbers look diluted purely because of water content. To compare foods fairly, nutrition guidelines convert to a **dry matter basis** (removing moisture from the calculation first), which is a more apples-to-apples comparison but isn't what's printed on the label itself.

## The AAFCO Statement: What It Confirms (and Doesn't)

Most commercial pet foods carry a statement referencing AAFCO (the Association of American Feed Control Officials) — something like "formulated to meet the nutritional levels established by the AAFCO Dog/Cat Food Nutrient Profiles" or "complete and balanced." This statement is confirming the food meets minimum/maximum nutrient thresholds for a given life stage (growth, adult maintenance, or "all life stages"). It's a genuinely useful baseline check — but it's a floor, not a feeding instruction, and it says nothing about how many calories are in a given serving.

## The Number Most Owners Skip: Calories Per 100g (or Per Cup/Can)

Buried in smaller text, sometimes on the side or back of the bag, is the **calorie content statement** — typically expressed as kcal per kilogram, and often also per cup or per can for convenience. This is the number that actually matters for portion control, and it's the one most owners never look at, relying instead on the front-of-bag feeding chart, which is calculated from a generic "average" pet.

This number varies more than people expect between products that look similar. Two "adult maintenance" dry foods from different brands can differ by 15–20% in calories per 100g — meaning the same *scoop size* could be correctly portioned for one and overfeeding (or underfeeding) by a meaningful margin for the other.

## Why This Number Is the Actual Input for Portion Math

Once you know a pet's total daily calorie target (from their weight, life stage, and activity level — see our guide on [calculating daily calorie needs](/blog/how-much-to-feed-dog-cat)), converting that target into a real-world amount to put in the bowl requires exactly one more piece of information: that food's calories per 100g. Everything else on the label is useful context, but this is the number the actual math runs on.

## A Few Label-Reading Pitfalls

- **"Cup" measurements aren't standardized across brands.** A "cup" scoop for one food's density isn't necessarily the same actual gram weight as another food's "cup," even for the same nominal cup size — weighing food in grams is more reliable than scooping by volume.
- **Treats often don't carry a calorie-per-100g statement at all**, or list it inconsistently, which is part of why treats are easy to accidentally over-budget.
- **"Light" or "weight management" labeling isn't standardized to a specific calorie threshold** the way some other claims are — always check the actual calorie content statement rather than trusting the marketing term alone.
- **Ingredient lists are ordered by pre-cooking weight**, which can make a lower-quality ingredient appear higher on the list than its actual contribution to the finished food.

## The Bottom Line

Most of a pet food label is composition information, useful for comparing quality between products but not directly usable for portioning. The single number that actually drives correct portion sizes — calories per 100g (or per cup/can) — is often the smallest text on the package, and skipping it is exactly why front-of-bag feeding charts and manual scooping tend to drift away from a pet's actual calorie needs over time.
