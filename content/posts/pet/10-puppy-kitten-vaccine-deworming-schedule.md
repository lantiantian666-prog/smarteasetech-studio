# Puppy and Kitten Vaccine and Deworming Schedule — What Happens When, and Why It's Easy to Lose Track

New puppy and kitten owners are usually handed a vaccine schedule at the first vet visit — and then, a few weeks later, asked to remember exactly when the *next* one is due, on their own, without a reminder card in front of them. It's a system that depends heavily on memory, and it's one of the most common places pet care quietly falls through the cracks.

## Why It's a Multi-Dose Series, Not a Single Shot

Puppies and kittens are typically born with some temporary immunity passed from their mother, which fades gradually over the first few months of life — but at an unpredictable rate for any individual animal. Because of this, core vaccines aren't given as a single dose; they're given as a **series**, usually starting around 6–8 weeks of age and repeating every 3–4 weeks until roughly 16 weeks old, specifically to make sure at least one dose lands after the maternal immunity has fully faded and the vaccine can actually take effect.

This is also why a puppy or kitten isn't considered fully protected after just the first shot — skipping or delaying a dose in the middle of the series doesn't just delay protection, it can mean the series has to be adjusted or restarted depending on how much time has passed.

## Deworming Follows Its Own, Separate Schedule

Deworming is often bundled into the same vet visits as vaccines, but it runs on its own logic: young puppies and kittens are commonly dewormed starting as early as 2 weeks of age and repeated every 2–3 weeks through the early months, because intestinal parasites are extremely common in young animals and a single dose typically doesn't clear a full parasite life cycle. This is a separate tracking need from the vaccine series, even though it often happens at overlapping visits.

## Why This Specific Schedule Is Hard to Track From Memory

A few things make this particular schedule more error-prone than most recurring pet care tasks:

- **The intervals are short and irregular** — every 3–4 weeks doesn't line up with any calendar rhythm like "every month" or "every two weeks," making it easy to lose count of which dose is next.
- **It only happens once, for a few months, and then never again in the same way** — unlike an annual booster, there's no habit built up around it before the series ends.
- **Two different schedules (vaccines and deworming) are running in parallel**, on different intervals, which doubles the chance of losing track of one of them.
- **Multi-person households compound the problem** — if more than one person might take the pet to the vet, it's easy for both people to assume the other is tracking the next due date.

## Adult Boosters Don't End the Need to Track This

After the initial puppy/kitten series, most core vaccines move to a periodic booster schedule — commonly annual or every three years depending on the vaccine and local veterinary guidance. It's a lower-frequency problem at that point, but the underlying issue is the same: a due date set months or years in advance, with nothing prompting you until it's already overdue.
