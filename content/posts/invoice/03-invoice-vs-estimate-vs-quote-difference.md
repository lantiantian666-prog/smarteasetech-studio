"Should I send an invoice or an estimate?" It's one of the most common points of confusion for new freelancers and contractors, and the terms get used interchangeably so often that it's easy to lose track of what actually separates them. Getting this right matters — using the wrong document at the wrong stage of a project can confuse clients about what they owe and when, and in some cases can create real accounting headaches down the line.

Here's a clear breakdown of each document, when to send it, and how they connect to one another.

## Quote: A Fixed Price, Offered Upfront

A quote is a fixed-price offer for a specific, well-defined scope of work. When you send a quote, you're telling the client "this exact job will cost this exact amount" — and once they accept it, that price is typically locked in, barring any changes to the agreed scope.

Quotes work best for jobs where the scope is fully known in advance: installing a specific fixture, designing a logo with a defined number of concepts, building a website from an agreed sitemap. Because the price is fixed, quotes carry more risk for the person issuing them — if the job turns out to be more complicated than expected, you generally can't charge more without a formal change order.

## Estimate: An Approximate Price, Subject to Change

An estimate is a *close approximation* of what a job will cost, explicitly understood by both parties to be non-binding. Estimates are the right tool when the full scope isn't knowable until work begins — a renovation where hidden issues might surface once walls are opened, a consulting engagement where the number of hours needed depends on what's discovered along the way.

A good estimate still itemizes expected costs (labor, materials, line items) so the client has a clear sense of where their money is going — it's just understood that the final invoice may differ if the scope shifts.

## Invoice: A Request for Payment for Completed Work

An invoice is what you send *after* work is finished (or at agreed milestones), formally requesting payment. Unlike a quote or estimate, an invoice is not a proposal — it's a commercial document confirming that specific goods or services were delivered and payment is now due.

This is the key distinction: **quotes and estimates come before the work; invoices come after (or during, for milestone billing).** An invoice should always reflect what was actually delivered, not just what was originally proposed.

## How They Connect: The Estimate-to-Invoice Workflow

In practice, the cleanest workflow looks like this:

1. **Send an estimate** (or quote) outlining the proposed work and price.
2. **Client accepts** — verbally, by email, or by signing off directly on the document.
3. **Complete the work.**
4. **Convert that same estimate into an invoice**, adjusting line items only if the actual scope changed, and send it for payment.

The biggest time-waster in this workflow is re-typing everything from scratch when it's time to invoice — re-entering client details, re-adding every line item, redoing the math. ## Quick Reference

| Document | Sent... | Price is... | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quote | Before work begins | Fixed | Lock in a price for a defined scope |
| Estimate | Before work begins | Approximate | Give a close-enough number when scope may shift |
| Invoice | After work is completed (or at milestones) | Final | Formally request payment |

Understanding this distinction — and using each document at the right moment — is one of the simplest ways to look more professional to clients and avoid billing disputes before they start.
